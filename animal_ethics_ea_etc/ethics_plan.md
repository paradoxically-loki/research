# A Thematic Self-Study Plan for Moral Philosophy

Built for: some prior exposure, thematic organization, secondary sources before primary texts.

---

## Phase 0: Get the Map (1-2 weeks)

Before splitting into themes, get one book that shows you the whole territory. This is your spine text, the thing you return to when you're lost.

- **The Elements of Moral Philosophy** by James Rachels (later editions with Stuart Rachels). The standard entry point for a reason: each chapter takes one theory or problem, states the strongest version of it, then the strongest objections. Clear, fair, opinionated where it counts. Read it cover to cover before anything else.

---

## Phase 1: Normative Ethics — What Makes an Action Right? (2-3 months)

This is what most people mean by "ethics." Three major traditions get full treatment; two more get a lighter pass.

### 1. Consequentialism / Utilitarianism
- **Utilitarianism: A Very Short Introduction** by Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek and Peter Singer. Short, sharp, written by people who actually hold the view, so you get it argued for rather than just described.
- **Utilitarianism** by John Stuart Mill (1861). The primary text. Short and genuinely readable, none of the difficulty of Kant. Pay attention to how Mill tries to answer the "utilitarianism is just for pigs" objection in Chapter 2.

*Honest note: this is the easiest theory to grasp and the easiest to demolish with a thought experiment. Don't let the ease fool you into dismissing it; the hard version is much harder to kill than the cartoon version.*

### 2. Deontology
- **Kantian Ethics** by Allen Wood. A proper secondary treatment, systematic and not dumbed down, but written for someone who hasn't read Kant yet.
- **Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals** by Immanuel Kant (1785), Mary Gregor translation (Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy). The primary text.

*Honest note: the Groundwork is genuinely hard. Not hard like a difficult novel, hard like reading a proof where you have to hold four definitions in your head at once. Budget more time than feels reasonable. Reread paragraphs. This is normal, not a sign you're bad at this.*

### 3. Virtue Ethics
- **On Virtue Ethics** by Rosalind Hursthouse. The standard contemporary case for the view, written by its best living defender.
- **Nicomachean Ethics** by Aristotle, Terence Irwin translation (Hackett). The primary text and the origin of the whole tradition. Irwin's notes are worth reading alongside the text, not skipped.

*Honest note: this is the theory most people find intuitively appealing on first contact and most people struggle to actually apply. "What would a virtuous person do" is a satisfying answer until you try to use it to settle a real dilemma. Sit with that tension rather than explaining it away.*

### 4. Contractualism and Care Ethics (lighter pass)
- Read the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on **Contractualism** first, it's a genuinely good overview and there's no equivalent short secondary book.
- **What We Owe to Each Other** by T.M. Scanlon (1998). Dense, but dip into the introduction and first chapter rather than committing to the whole thing yet.
- **The Ethics of Care: Personal, Political, and Global** by Virginia Held. More accessible than Scanlon, and a genuinely different starting question: not "what rules apply" but "what does responsiveness to particular others require."

## Phase 2: Metaethics — What Is Morality, Actually? (1-2 months)

Now the theories you just learned get put in question. Is any of this true? Are moral claims even the kind of thing that can be true?

- **Whatever Happened to Good and Evil?** by Russ Shafer-Landau. Written for a general audience, walks through relativism, error theory, expressivism, and realism without assuming you've taken a class.
- **An Introduction to Contemporary Metaethics** by Alexander Miller. More rigorous, save this for once Shafer-Landau feels comfortable rather than reading them back to back.
- **Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong** by J.L. Mackie (1977). Primary text, and a good one to start primary reading in metaethics with, since Mackie writes clearly and argues for a genuinely startling position: that all positive moral claims are false (error theory).
- **Principia Ethica** by G.E. Moore (1903), specifically the chapter on the "open question argument." Historically the essay that made 20th century metaethics happen. The prose is dated and a bit of a slog; you can get the argument from a good secondary summary and only read the original passage itself.

---

## Phase 3: Applied Ethics — Putting the Frameworks to Work (ongoing)

This is where theory earns its keep. Pick issues you actually care about rather than working through all of them.

- **Practical Ethics** by Peter Singer. Covers animals, poverty, abortion, euthanasia, all argued from a stated framework and engaging seriously with objections. The most readable working philosopher on this list; if you want momentum, start here.
- **A Defense of Abortion** by Judith Jarvis Thomson (1971 essay). The violinist thought experiment. Short, famous, and a genuinely good model of how a single thought experiment can do real philosophical work.
- **The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of Double Effect** by Philippa Foot (1967 essay). The trolley problem starts here, not with Thomson, contrary to popular belief.
- **Famine, Affluence, and Morality** by Peter Singer (1972 essay). Short, the founding document of what became the effective altruism movement.
- **Doing Good Better** by William MacAskill. If the Singer essay grabs you, this is the accessible book-length follow-through.

## Reference Tools (keep these open while you read)

- **Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy** (plato.stanford.edu). The actual gold standard. Rigorous, free, peer-reviewed, and this is where you go for the historical "who's responding to whom" context I mentioned above.
- **1000-Word Philosophy** (1000wordphilosophy.com). Short, accessible essays on specific arguments and philosophers. Good for filling gaps without a huge time investment.
- **Aeon** (aeon.co). Longer essays, more of a magazine feel, philosophy alongside other ideas writing. Uneven but the good pieces are very good.
- **Practical Ethics** blog, Oxford Uehiro Centre. Academic philosophers writing about current events through an applied ethics lens. Good for seeing the theory used in real time.

## Podcasts

- **Philosophy Bites**. Short episodes, one philosopher, one topic. The best low-commitment entry point of anything on this list.
- **Very Bad Wizards**. Casual tone, substantive content, ethics and moral psychology.
- **Hi-Phi Nation**. Narrative, well produced, a different format than the interview style of the other two.

---

## Where to Go Deeper Later (don't start here)

Once the above feels solid, these are the texts that define the current state of the field:

- **On What Matters** (Vols. 1-2) by Derek Parfit. Long, ambitious, tries to show consequentialism, Kantianism, and contractualism converge more than they seem to. Not a beginner book.
- **The Sources of Normativity** by Christine Korsgaard. A serious contemporary defense of Kantian ethics against the "why should I be moral at all" question.
- **Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy** by Bernard Williams. A skeptical, genuinely contrarian book about what moral theory can and can't do. Good corrective after you've absorbed the theories earnestly.
- **Modern Moral Philosophy** by G.E.M. Anscombe (1958 essay). The essay that arguably restarted virtue ethics as a live option. Short and worth reading once you know enough to see what she's attacking.

---

## Suggested Pace

At roughly 3-4 hours a week, this is a 9-12 month project done properly. That's not a deadline, it's a sanity check: if you're moving much faster than that, you're probably skimming rather than reading, especially through the Kant and metaethics sections. Slower is fine. This is a hobby, not a syllabus with a final exam.
